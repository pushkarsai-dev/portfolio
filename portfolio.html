Academic Background

From curiosity to craft

I arrived at NC State drawn by the Honors Program's promise of close mentorship and original inquiry. From my first semester, I sought out research experiences rather than waiting for them to find me. By sophomore year, I was assisting in the Chen Lab; by junior year, I had designed and defended my own independent project examining the role of Wnt signaling pathway components in early embryonic morphogenesis in C. elegans.

My coursework has been deliberately broad: genetics, biochemistry, developmental biology, bioethics, science writing, and an elective in computational biology have together given me a vocabulary for thinking across disciplinary boundaries. I believe that the most interesting biological questions sit at the edges of categories.

Research Focus

Developmental genetics & morphogenetic signaling

My primary research interest is understanding how cells interpret signaling gradients to make patterning decisions during embryonic development. In the Chen Lab, I have used CRISPR-Cas9 and RNAi approaches to investigate how mutations in frizzled receptor homologs affect cell polarity and tissue architecture in C. elegans embryos.

This work has taught me to move between detailed mechanistic questions and larger conceptual frameworks — a skill I expect to carry into doctoral work. I am particularly interested in how conserved developmental programs have been modified across phylogenies, and what those modifications reveal about the relationship between genotype and form.

Leadership Experience

Science is a community, not a solo act

Alongside my research, I have served as Vice President of the Biology Undergraduate Society, coordinating departmental speaker events and peer mentorship circles. I co-founded a weekly science communication workshop for pre-research undergraduates, and have spent two years tutoring first-generation college students through the NC State TRIO program.

These experiences have refined my understanding of equity in STEM. Mentoring students who lack the cultural capital that eases entry into research culture has made me attentive to how institutions shape opportunity, and committed to building more accessible pathways.

Future Goals

Toward a research career grounded in impact

I plan to pursue a Ph.D. in Developmental Biology or Genetics, with a long-term goal of running an independent research program at a research university. Equally important to me is maintaining a strong commitment to mentorship, science education, and communicating biology to public audiences.

I am drawn to programs that value collaboration, support early independence, and take seriously both the scientific and ethical dimensions of biological research.

Culminating Reflection

Finding the Shape of My Education

A reflection on intellectual growth, scientific identity, and the values I carry forward.

Contents

  • I. Introduction
  • II. Becoming a Scientist
  • III. The Ethics of Inquiry
  • IV. Teaching as Learning
  • V. Leadership & Community
  • VI. Looking Forward
Approximately 2,500 words

I. Introduction

When I arrived at NC State in the fall of 2021, I thought I knew what a biologist was. I imagined a person in a white coat, working alone at a bench, producing truths that the world would eventually receive. I was wrong in almost every particular, and correcting those errors has been the real substance of my undergraduate education.

This portfolio reflects four years of learning to be wrong well — to treat failure as information, confusion as a starting point, and difficulty as an invitation rather than an obstacle. The artifacts gathered here are not primarily evidence of success. They are evidence of growth, and they are organized around the dimensions of that growth: scientific research and innovation, teaching and mentorship, leadership and service, and interdisciplinary inquiry. Together, they trace the shape of an education I could not have planned in advance.

II. Becoming a Scientist

My first exposure to genuine scientific uncertainty came in the spring of my sophomore year, when I joined Dr. Priya Chen's developmental genetics lab as an unpaid volunteer. I had taken two semesters of biology and one of chemistry. I did not yet know how to hold a pipette without shaking.

What I discovered in that lab was not the clean, linear process that my coursework had implied. It was messy, recursive, and humbling. My first assigned protocol — a simple RNAi feeding experiment — failed four times before I understood that "failure" was the wrong word for it. Each attempt taught me something. The question was whether I had the patience and rigor to notice what it was teaching me.

By junior year, I had designed my own project: an investigation of how mutations in frizzled receptor homologs affect cell polarity and tissue architecture in C. elegans embryos. This project required me to integrate knowledge from developmental biology, genetics, cell biology, and basic statistics — and to tolerate long stretches of ambiguous data that resisted clean interpretation. The experience of designing and executing an original experiment, from hypothesis through analysis, changed how I understand knowledge itself. Science is not the accumulation of facts. It is a discipline of calibrated uncertainty, practiced in community.

The artifacts in Section I of this portfolio — including my research symposium poster, laboratory notebook excerpts, and statistical analysis report — document this transformation. They are imperfect documents, and I have chosen not to hide their imperfections. The crossed-out entries in my lab notebook, the initial visualizations that misrepresented the data, the literature review draft with its marginal corrections — these are the real record of how scientific thinking develops.

III. The Ethics of Inquiry

In the spring of my junior year, I enrolled in BIO 495: Bioethics, expecting it to be a peripheral complement to my "real" scientific work. I was wrong. The course forced me to confront questions that my laboratory training had been silently bracketing: Who benefits from the research being done? Who decides what gets studied? What do we owe the organisms we use, the communities from which we draw subjects, and the future people who will inherit the consequences of our choices?

My seminar paper on germline genome editing pushed me to think carefully about the relationship between scientific capability and social wisdom. We can, in principle, heritably edit human embryos. The question of whether and when we should is not a scientific question. It requires a framework that my biology training alone could not supply. Working through that paper — reading philosophy, bioethics, and science and technology studies alongside the technical literature — taught me that scientific competence without ethical reflection is dangerous, and that ethical reflection without scientific literacy is ineffective. The combination is what responsible research requires.

This interdisciplinary encounter has permanently changed how I approach my own work. When I think about the experiments I want to do in graduate school, I now also think about the ethical dimensions of the model systems I use, the questions I choose to pursue, and the communities that might be affected by the knowledge I produce.

IV. Teaching as Learning

I became a teaching assistant for BIO 201 laboratory in the fall of my junior year, partly to earn money and partly out of a vague sense that teaching would be useful. What I did not anticipate was how fundamentally it would change my understanding of the material I thought I already knew.

Explaining cell division to a first-year student who is genuinely confused about it requires a different kind of understanding than being able to answer exam questions. It requires knowing why the confusing parts are confusing, where the conceptual sticking points are, and how to find multiple routes through the same idea. I discovered that I had relied, in my own learning, on forms of familiarity that were not genuine comprehension. Teaching exposed those gaps and forced me to fill them.

The TRIO tutoring program deepened this further. Working with first-generation college students who were navigating not just course material but an entirely unfamiliar academic culture taught me to see higher education from the outside. Many of these students were brilliant — more resourceful and determined than most of their peers — but they lacked the informal knowledge that shapes how science is learned and who gets to do it. Watching them succeed, and sometimes watching institutions fail them, clarified my own commitments around equity in STEM more than any reading could have.

V. Leadership & Community

I am, by temperament, more comfortable alone at a bench than at a podium. Serving as Vice President of the Biology Undergraduate Society for two years was an act of deliberate self-expansion — a decision to practice skills that did not come naturally to me because I believed they were important.

The most significant thing I contributed during my tenure was not any particular event, but a structural change: I co-designed a peer mentorship program that paired upper-division students with first- and second-year students seeking research experience. The program now serves 60 students per year and has connected many students with labs they would not otherwise have discovered. Building it required convincing skeptical faculty, managing volunteer coordinators, and sustaining enthusiasm across a full academic year. None of those tasks appear on my transcript.

The Science Communication Workshop I co-founded was born from a related observation: that many undergraduates who wanted to do research did not know how to talk about science — how to read a paper, what to say in a lab meeting, how to ask questions that would not expose them to embarrassment. The workshop addressed those gaps directly, meeting students where they were and treating the acquisition of academic culture not as something to be ashamed of not having, but as a learnable skill.

These experiences taught me that leadership is fundamentally about clarity: clarity about what matters, what a group can accomplish, and how individuals can contribute. It is much less about charisma and much more about sustained attention.

VI. Looking Forward

I enter graduate school with a clearer sense of what I believe and a more honest accounting of what I do not yet know. I know that I want to study the relationship between signaling dynamics and morphogenetic outcomes. I know that I want to mentor students and build lab cultures that take the psychological safety of trainees seriously. I know that I want to communicate science to audiences outside the academy, and that I care about who gets to do science and under what conditions.

What I do not know is which specific questions will hold me for the next decade. That uncertainty no longer frightens me the way it once did. I have learned, over four years, that the questions that sustain a scientific career cannot be chosen in advance. They are discovered through practice, through the accumulation of small technical skills and large conceptual failures, through conversations with collaborators and mentors and students, and through the slow realization that one particular corner of the unknown is the most interesting place in the world to spend a life.

This portfolio is my best attempt to document that learning — not as a polished product, but as an honest record. The artifacts are imperfect. The reflections are incomplete. They are, in that sense, accurate representations of where I am: a person who has learned a great deal and who is, for that reason, more aware than ever of how much she does not yet know.

I am ready to begin.

Alexandra J. Morrison
